           SUMMARY  
          This bill establishes a California Water Quality Monitoring  
          Council (Council) to be administered by the State Water  
          Resources Control Board (SWRCB) to improve coordination of water  
          quality data collection programs and to ensure sufficient  
          information is collected to track water quality improvements and  
          the efficacy of programs designed to achieve clean water and  
          healthy ecosystems.  Specifically, this bill:
          1)Requires, by December 1, 2006, CalEPA and the Resources Agency  
            to enter into an MOU to establish the council, and requires  
            CalEPA, starting December 1, 2008, to submit a biennial audit  
            evaluating the effectiveness of the monitoring and assessment  
            network developed and implemented by the council.
          2)Requires the SWRCB to place, on its website, a public  
            information file on water quality research, standards,  
            regulation, enforcement, and related matters.
           FISCAL EFFECT  
          1)Moderate costs, perhaps $250,000 every two years starting in  
            FY 2008-09, to CalEPA to conduct the water quality monitoring  
            audit.  (GF or Waste Discharge Permit Fund.)
          2)Minor ongoing costs, likely less than $25,000 starting in FY  
            2006-07, to the SWRCB to gather and place water  
            quality-related information on its website.
          3)Substantial GF cost pressures, in the range of $5 million  
            annually starting in FY 2007-08, to the SWRCB to administer an

            to improve tracking of water quality-related information.

           COMMENTS  
           1)Rationale  .  The author contends that monitoring of the quality  
            of the state's waters, whether along the coast in the near  
            shore environment or lakes, rivers, and other freshwater  
            bodies, is haphazard, incomplete, and inconsistent.  This  
            bill, by establishing the council, is intended to coordinate  
            statewide efforts to monitor water quality and to achieve a  
            higher level of consistency in monitoring methods and  
            information tracking.  By doing so, the author hopes to expand  
            the scope of monitoring so as to adequately keep track of  
            water quality in all the state's waters.
           2)Background .  In its most recent monitoring report to the U.S.  
            EPA, California's water agencies admitted they could only  
            report on the quality of 22% of it coastal shoreline, 34% of  
            its lakes and reservoirs, and 15% of its rivers and streams.   
            These low percentages are a function of the vastness of  
            California's water resources, the decline in state funding  
            devoted to water monitoring activities, and the lack of  
            coordination between different entities that conduct  
            monitoring and different methods used.  
          In addition to not being able to determine if the quality of  
            many bodies of water has been compromised, it is often not  
            possible to determine if new water quality initiatives funded  
            by bond proceeds or significant discharger fees are effective  
            in improving water quality.
           3)Short-Term Costs vs. Long-Term Savings  .  While requiring the  
            council and the SWRCB to expand its efforts to monitor the  
            quality of the state's waters would require a substantial GF  
            expenditures, a statewide, coordinated monitoring network and  
            tracking system is likely to better position the SWRCB and  
            other regulators of water quality to concentrate on degraded  
            bodies of water and to focus fiscal resources on priority  
            water quality problems.
