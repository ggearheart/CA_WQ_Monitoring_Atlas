  SUMMARY  :   Requires the State Water Resources Control Board  
(Board) to conduct a series of water quality studies based on  
the recommendations of the advisory group(s) and report its  
findings to the Legislature by specified dates as the basis for  
implementing a total maximum daily load program pursuant to the  
federal Water Pollution Control Act, commonly known as the Clean  
Water Act (CWA).
  The Senate amendments   revise the Assembly version of this bill  
from a statement of legislative intent and instead:
1)Require the Board to convene one or more advisory groups to  
  assist in evaluating the structure and efficacy of the  
  impaired water bodies program, specifying the membership and  
  procedures of the advisory group(s).
2)Require the Board to report to the Legislature, in accordance  
  with specified dates, on the structure and effectiveness of  
  its water quality program as it relates to CWA, taking into  
  consideration the recommendations of the advisory group(s).
3)Require the Board to report to the Legislature, in accordance  
  with specified dates, on the regional water quality control  
  boards' current surface water quality monitoring programs for  
  designing a comprehensive surface water monitoring program  
  based on specified elements.
4)Specify that nothing in this bill affects the authority of the  
  regional water quality control boards.
  EXISTING LAW  requires:
1)The Board to comply with applicable regulations adopted by the  
  United States Environmental Protection Agency (U.S. EPA).  The  
  Porter-Cologne Act authorizes the Board to fulfill the

  requirements of CWA.  CWA requires that the state issue a  
  biennial report on the results of statewide monitoring of  
  impaired and unimpaired waters.  California has broad

  AS PASSED BY THE ASSEMBLY  ,  this bill stated that it was the  
intent of the Legislature to implement a Total Maximum Daily  
Load (TMDL) program consistent with, and no less stringent than  
CWA. 
  FISCAL EFFECT  :   According to the Senate Appropriations  
Committee, this measure would require up to $100,000 for the  
advisory group(s) and an additional, unspecified amount, to  
conform with reporting requirements.
  COMMENTS  :  This bill was substantially amended in the Senate.   
The Assembly-approved provisions of this bill were revised in  
the Senate to elaborate language that is consistent with prior  
Assembly actions.
TMDLs have been required by Section 303(d) of CWA since it was  
first passed in 1972.  It is only in recent years, however, that  
any TMDLs have been established.  Section 303(d) of CWA requires  
states to identify all the water bodies that do not meet  
applicable water quality standards, and for those "impaired"  
water bodies, states must establish TMDLs.   TMDL calculates the  
total amount of a given pollutant that can be released into a  
water body before the water quality standard will be violated.   
TMDL distributes waste total amount of permissible discharges  
among all dischargers of that pollutant to a particular  
waterway.  The portion of a receiving water's loading capacity  
allocated to a point source is known as a waste load allocation  
(WLA).  WLAs are enforced through conditions inserted into waste  
discharge permits.  The portion allocated to a nonpoint source  
or naturally occurring pollutants is known as a load allocation  
(LA) and is to be enforced through the state's nonpoint source

management program.  TMDL is the sum of all WLAs and LAs.   
Permit-writers are supposed to assure that multiple sources of  
pollutants (i.e., point, nonpoint, and background) will not  
exceed the biological integrity of the water body.  
This section of CWA was essentially ignored for years until  
environmental lawsuits forced U.S. EPA to issue lists of  
impaired water bodies and to establish TMDLs where states failed  
to do so.  Despite the fact that numerous California's water  
bodies are subject to consent decrees to develop TMDLs or are  
the subject of notices of intent to file lawsuits, there are no  
prescribed rules for establishing and implementing TMDLs. 
  U.S. EPA is currently in the process of proposing regulations  
governing the development of TMDLs.  A raft rule was expected in  
June 1999 and a final rule is anticipated by early 2000.  If  
California adopts rules that are inconsistent with the federal  
regulations, such regulations may be contested and challenged as  
inconsistent with CWA.  Any delays could pose a problem for the  
state, especially where court orders have set deadlines for the  
adoption of TMDLs.
The state currently has more than 400 impaired water bodies that  
must be addressed under CWA.  To move forward with establishing  
a TMDL program, the state must begin gathering scientific data  
on the nature and sources of impairments to state water bodies  
as well as establishing ground rules for setting forth  
priorities regarding impaired water bodies and pollutants,  
allocating responsibility to reduce pollutant loans, and  
evaluating various proposed control strategies.
For a more specific review of issues surrounding the measure as  
introduced, see the Environmental Safety and Toxic Materials'  
committee analysis dated April 13, 1999.
