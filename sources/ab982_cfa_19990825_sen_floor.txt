  SUBJECT  :    Water quality:  federal Clean Water Act  
     compliance:
            impaired water bodies
  SOURCE  :     Author
  DIGEST  :    This bill provides a framework for the  
implementation of the state's impaired water bodies program  
required pursuant to the federal Clean Water Act.
  ANALYSIS  :    Existing law:
1.Under federal Clean Water Act (33 U.S.C.A. Sections  
  1251-1387):
                                                 CONTINUED
                                                      AB 982
                                                       Page  
2
   (a)Requires the state to identify those waters within  
      its boundaries which are not sufficiently protected  
      by state and federal water quality standards  
      (identified as "impaired water bodies"), to rank such  
      waters based upon the severity of their pollution,  
      and to establish total maximum daily loads (TMDL's)  
      for pollutants found in those waters.
   (b)Authorizes any citizen to commence a civil action  
      against the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)  
      for the enforcement of any water quality standard  
      established under the federal Act.
2.Under the Porter-Cologne Water Quality Control Act  
  (Division 7 (commencing with Section 13000) of the Water  
  Code):
   (a)Generally requires the State Water Resources Control  
      Board (SWRCB) and the nine regional water quality  
      control boards (RWQCB's) to protect and maintain the  
      beneficial uses of the waters of the state.
   (b)Delegates, with several exceptions, responsibility  
      for overseeing state compliance with the federal  
      Clean Water Act to the State and regional water  
      boards.
   (c)Requires each RWQCB to adopt a water quality control  
      plan for its region which provide for the reasonable  
      protection of the beneficial uses of waters in that  
      region and the prevention of nuisance.
This bill:
1.Requires the SWRCB to convene one or more advisory groups  
  to assist in the evaluation of the program structure and  
  effectiveness of the impaired water bodies program  
  required to be implemented by the board pursuant to the  
  federal Clean Water Act, and specifies the membership and  
  procedures for operation of the advisory group or groups.  
   Requires the SWRCB to provide public notice on its  
  website of any meetings of the advisory group or groups.
                                                      AB 982

2.Requires, on or before November 30, 2000 and annually  
  thereafter until November 30, 2002, the SWRCB to report  
  to the Legislature on the structure and effectiveness of  
  its water quality program as it relates to the federal  
  Act, and requires the board to take into consideration  
  the recommendations of the advisory group or groups  
  established pursuant to (1) above.
3.On or before November 30, 2000, requires the SWRCB to  
  report to the Legislature on its and regional quality  
  control boards' current surface water quality monitoring  
  programs for the purpose of designing a comprehensive  
  surface water monitoring program for the state, and to  
  include specified elements in that report.
4.Specifies that nothing in the bill affects the authority  
  of the regional water quality control boards.
  Comments  
According to Senate Environmental Quality Committee  
analysis:
1. Purpose of Bill  .  According to the author's office, this  
  measure is intended to provide a framework for the  
  implementation of the state's impaired water bodies  
  program required pursuant to the federal Clean Water Act.  
   The author notes that, in response to federal  
  litigation, the US EPA and the state board have both been  
  compelled to move forward in order to comply with the  
  requirements of Section 3030(d) of the federal Act.  This  
  bill is intended to ensure that the Legislature is kept  
  apprised of that process and that various stakeholders  
  have input into the process.
2.  Impaired Water Bodies Program Described  .  The following  
  is taken from an April 1999 Report issued by the  
  California Research Bureau, entitled  TMDL's The  
  Revolution in Water Quality Regulation  (Ruffulo):
  "California's "modern" legal arrangements for cleaning up  
  its rivers, lakes, and coastal waters go back 30 years to  
  the state's Porter-Cologne Act and especially to the  
  federal Clean Water Act of 1972.  The main thrust of the  
                                                      AB 982
                                                       Page  
4
  CWA was to require cities and industries to clean up the  
  waste water they discharged from their "point sources,"  
  which were mostly sewer outfalls and an assortment of  
  other pipes and ditches.  This approach had much to  
  recommend it.  A sizeable portion of water pollution came  
  from these kinds of sources, the pollution was orderly in  
  the sense that it was already contained in a pipe or  
  channel, there was little ambiguity about who caused the  
  pollution, and workable treatment technologies existed.   
  There were complaints, it was expensive, but enormous  
  progress was made in cleaning up the state's surface  
  water.
  The Clean Water Act also included a second, conceptually  
  different and broader approach to cleaning up our water.   
  This second approach drew little attention when the CWA  
  was passed, or for more than a decade after that, until  
  it was "discovered" by environmental litigants and the  
  courts.  It required each state to assess the actual  
  water quality of each of its water bodies.  Even after  
  imposing stringent controls on point water pollution  
  sources, it is perhaps not surprising that most states  
  found that many water bodies were still somewhat  
  polluted.  The second front of the CWA seems to require  
  states, including California, to establish the total  
  amount of each category of pollutant that each water body  
  can absorb each day without becoming "polluted" (called  
  Total Maximum Daily Loading, or TMDL) and to somehow  
  ensure that that limit is not exceeded.
  Exactly how this TMDL requirement is going to work is far  
  from clear; almost everything about it is cloaked in  
  controversy or at least ambiguity.  However, the weight  
  of court decisions from around the country and especially  
  in California suggest the very real possibility that  
  water quality regulation is in the act of changing in  
  quite fundamental ways.  The most important change is  
  also the simplest: if water bodies must be made cleaner,  
  then one of two things must happen.  Either point  
  sources, such as municipalities and factories, must clean  
  up their discharges even further, even though additional  
  purity comes at great expense, or nonpoint sources, such  
  as farms, timber operators, and urban runoff, must be  
  subject to stringent water quality controls that they  
                                                      AB 982
                                                       Page  
5
  have avoided so far.
  TMDL requirements have the potential to greatly expand  
  the scope, impact, and economic cost of water quality  
  regulations, and could change the way that the  
  agriculture, forestry, and construction industries do  
  business.  Estimates of the regulatory cost of this  
  program range from $5 million to $1 billion, excluding  
  the costs to industries and urban areas of pollution  
  controls needed to meet more stringent standards.
3.  1999-2000 Budget Act Contains Funds and Reporting  
  Requirements for Impaired Water Bodies Program  .  The  
  1999-2000 enacted State Budget appropriated $9 million  
  for implementation of the impaired water bodies program  
  required pursuant to federal law and consent decrees.  It  
  also included several million dollars for increased water  
  quality monitoring.  Due to ongoing concerns related to  
  the SWRCB's general implementation of its core regulatory  
  program, the Legislature additionally established an  
  extensive series of reporting requirements on the board  
  as supplemental language in the budget.  Among other  
  things, these reporting requirements require the board to  
  keep the Legislature apprised of its actions in  
  implementing the program and how it relates to other  
  regulatory programs implemented by the board.
