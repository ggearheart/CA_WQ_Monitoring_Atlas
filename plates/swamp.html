SWAMP: AB 982 and the Surface Water Ambient Monitoring Program

In 1999 the Legislature asked the State Water Board a direct question: what would it take to monitor all of California's surface waters properly? The answer became the Surface Water Ambient Monitoring Program. The program was launched. The budget in the answer never was.

How it started

AB 982 was mainly about something else. California was being sued over its list of polluted waters, and the bill set up a public advisory process for that list. Before 1999, ambient monitoring had dwindled. The Legislative Analyst reported spending of about $12 million a year in the 1980s, paid from bond funds, and $2.8 million in 1999-00.

A Senate amendment added one more section to the bill. It did not create a program. It ordered a design for one:

"...the state board, on or before November 30, 2000, shall assess and report to the Legislature on the State Water Resources Control Board's and regional water control board's current surface water quality monitoring programs for the purpose of designing a proposal for a comprehensive surface water quality monitoring program for the state. The report shall include a proposal for the program, including steps and costs associated with developing the full program, cost of implementation..."Water Code §13192(a), as added by AB 982 (1999)

The Board delivered that report on time. It proposed a program covering every type of water body and every use the public makes of water. It also said about a quarter of the cost could come from redirecting existing work, which left an unmet need of roughly $44 to $87 million a year.

The section that created SWAMP has been repealed

In 2004 a clean-up bill removing old reporting requirements repealed §13192, because the one-time report had been delivered (). No statute replaced it with a standing mandate to run the program. Today SWAMP rests on a set of related authorities.

Clean Water Act §106(e)(1)Federal grants depend on the state carrying out a monitoring program
Water Code §13160Names the State Water Board as California's water pollution control agency for all Clean Water Act purposes

For a decision maker this is the key legal fact. SWAMP is authorized and expected, and federal funding assumes it. But no California statute says how large it must be or guarantees its budget.

What SWAMP does

ComponentThe question it answersHow
BioaccumulationIs it safe to eat the fish?Tests sport fish for mercury and other contaminants. The state's health scientists use the results to write fish consumption advisories.
Freshwater harmful algal bloomsIs it safe to swim, and for my dog to drink?Responds to bloom reports, tests for toxins, posts advisories.
BioassessmentAre our streams healthy?Surveys the insects and algae living in streams, which integrate conditions over months.
Stream Pollution TrendsAre pollutants getting better or worse?Tests sediment at the bottom of large watersheds for toxicity and chemicals, year after year.
Regional monitoringWhat is happening in this watershed?Each of the nine Regional Water Boards investigates local problems and supports its own permits and cleanup plans.
Data and quality assuranceCan the numbers be trusted and found?Shared methods, quality checks, the CEDEN data system, and support for community monitoring groups.

The federal yardstick

U.S. EPA judges every state monitoring program against ten elements. Several need steady funding more than new ideas.

  1. A long-term strategy
  2. Clear objectives
  3. A sound sampling design
  4. Core and supplemental indicators
  5. Quality assurance
  6. Data management
  7. Data analysis and assessment
  8. Reporting
  9. Periodic program evaluation
  10. General support and infrastructure

What reviewers have said

2005: the program's own strategy

Five years in, SWAMP reported it was funded at "approximately 7 percent of what is needed."

2014: joint review with U.S. EPA

SWAMP "is funded at a small fraction of the identified need." Funding "is currently about the same as when the program was first created in 2000," while "demands on the program have steadily increased, and costs have substantially increased."

Takeaway

AB 982 produced an honest price tag and a small program. Every review since has repeated the same finding: the design is sound and the funding is a fraction of what the design assumed.
