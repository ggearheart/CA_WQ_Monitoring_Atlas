Plate I

Why Monitor: the regulator's own view of the water

Agencies that protect a shared resource need to know its condition. A public health department tracks disease. A central bank tracks prices. A water quality agency tracks rivers, lakes and coastal waters. This plate covers the basics before the atlas turns to California's statutes.

Two kinds of monitoring

Most water quality sampling in California is done by permit holders, at their own discharge points, to show they are meeting their permits. That is compliance monitoring. It answers one question: is this facility following its rules?

Ambient monitoring measures the water body itself, away from any single pipe. It answers a different question: is the river healthy, whatever the cause? Only ambient monitoring can see the combined effect of every source at once, including farms, streets, wildfires, old mines and a changing climate, none of which report on themselves.

Six reasons governments do this themselves

1. You cannot manage what you have not measured

Standards, permits and cleanup plans all start from a statement about the water's current condition. Without measurements that statement is a guess.

2. The law requires it

The federal Clean Water Act will not fund a state water program that is not monitoring its waters. California has accepted that role (Water Code §13160).

3. Nobody else has the incentive

Clean water information is a public good. Everyone benefits, no one can be charged for using it, so no private party will produce it at the scale needed.

4. Independence

Permit holders measure their own pipes. An agency that relies only on self-reported data has no independent check on whether the rules are working.

5. Early warning

Toxic algae, new chemicals and post-fire runoff do not come with a permit. A standing network is how they get noticed before people are harmed.

6. Accountability for spending

The public spends billions on treatment plants and restoration. Ambient data is the only way to show whether that money improved the water.

This is not unique to water

Almost every regulatory or stewardship agency runs a surveillance program on the thing it protects, separate from the reports it collects from regulated parties.

Where the data goes

Ambient data feeds a repeating cycle. Each step depends on the one before it.

MonitorSample the water, fish and stream life.
AssessCompare results to standards. Is it safe to swim, fish, drink?
List and planName impaired waters and set cleanup targets.
ActWrite permits, fund restoration, post health advisories.
CheckMonitor again. Did the action work?
Takeaway

Ambient monitoring is the one data stream the Water Boards own that does not come from the regulated community. It is what lets the agency say, on its own authority, whether California's waters are getting better or worse.
