Risks: what it costs to find out late

An agency that monitors too little does not stop making decisions. It makes them with less information. The research describes five ways that goes wrong.

1. Flying blind

The first risk is simply not knowing. Auditors in several countries have found water agencies unable to describe the condition of most of the waters they are responsible for.

New Zealand's independent environment commissioner put it this way in 2019: "huge gaps in data and knowledge undermine our stewardship of the environment" (). A 2004 federal audit found that many organizations collect water data, yet officials still lack what they need, and recommended a lead coordinating body, which is the role SB 1070 later gave California's Monitoring Council (). The World Bank titled its 2019 global review of water pollution Quality Unknown (). Hydrologists have warned since 2001 that monitoring networks worldwide are shrinking (; ; ).

2. Wrong calls, in both directions

With few samples, an agency will sometimes declare a clean water polluted and a polluted water clean. Both mistakes cost money.

False alarm

A water body is listed as impaired when it is not. A cleanup plan is written, permits are tightened, and public and private money is spent on a problem that does not exist. A statistical review of the standard federal listing rule found it "has a high type I error rate," meaning frequent false alarms.

Missed problem

A water body is passed as healthy when it is not. People keep swimming and fishing, and the problem grows until it is more expensive to fix.

4. Thin networks can be gamed

Where monitoring is sparse or controlled by the regulated party, the numbers bend.

China: location is everything

Polluters just upstream of a government water monitoring station cut emissions by more than 57 percent compared with those just downstream, where no one was measuring.

China: who holds the instrument

When air monitors were automated so local officials could no longer adjust results, reported pollution rose 35 percent immediately and stayed there.

An ambient network that the agency runs itself, with enough stations that no one can plan around them, is the protection against both.

5. Monitoring badly

The literature is just as clear that poor monitoring is its own risk. Programs with too few samples to detect a change are "dangerous because [they create] the illusion that something useful has been done" (). Programs that collect data no one analyzes become "data-rich but information-poor" (). And without long records, each generation of managers accepts the degraded condition it inherits as normal ().

This matters for budget decisions. A cut that leaves a program too thin to detect trends does not save most of the money. It spends what remains on data that cannot answer the question.

Takeaway

The cost of not monitoring is rarely visible in the year the budget is cut. It shows up later as a surprise: an advisory that came too late, a cleanup plan aimed at the wrong water, or a contaminant that had been there for years.
