Next Five Years: SWAMP priorities for 2027 to 2032

The earlier plates explain how SWAMP arrived at a $2.2 million contract budget. This one looks forward. The program's draft strategic priorities for 2027 to 2032 set out what it intends to do with the resources it has, and where it wants to be ready if resources return.

How to read the plan

Two things stand out for a decision maker.

It is a plan for a constrained program

Very little in it expands field monitoring. Most projects improve how the program is run, how its data is shared, and how its results are explained. New kinds of monitoring appear as feasibility studies. That is what responsible planning looks like after a 62 percent cut.

It starts by asking what people need

Three separate needs assessments are planned: for the Water Boards' own regulatory programs, for communities, and for California Native American Tribes. The answers are meant to decide what gets monitored when money allows.

The four themes

Theme 1

Program management

Run a leaner program well, and be able to respond quickly when a water quality problem appears.

  • Write a funding framework. Document SWAMP's funding history and successes, state the needs, and identify a wider mix of funding options.
  • Set a strategy for DNA-based methods across the Water Boards.
  • Show, with data, the support services the office provides to other Water Board programs.
  • Put files, field equipment, web pages and project tracking in order.
Theme 2

Monitoring and assessment

Produce the data that core Water Board programs, communities and Tribes actually need.

  • Three needs assessments: Water Board programs, communities, Tribes.
  • Feasibility of post-fire monitoring, within existing statewide programs or as a new one, with the Monitoring Council.
  • Feasibility of monitoring plastics and trash.
  • Feasibility of community monitoring partnerships and a Tribal monitoring support program.
  • Confirm which reference streams go dry part of the year, to prepare for assessing such streams statewide.
Theme 3

Data quality and management

Make SWAMP's information trustworthy and easy to get.

  • Rebuild the statewide data system (CEDEN 2.0).
  • Publish plain data summaries for each statewide program on California's open data portal.
  • Modernize the harmful algal bloom map and data products, with equity at the center.
  • Write field methods, quality standards and data submission rules for DNA-based sampling.
Theme 4

Communication and engagement

Help the Board and the public understand what SWAMP data says and how it is used.

  • Bring SWAMP results to Board workshops as information items.
  • Map how each type of SWAMP data flows into decisions.
  • Fact sheets, story maps and web-based reports in place of long technical documents.
  • Annual data symposiums, and support for community science groups.
  • Support the Board's proposed climate change resolution.

How the plan connects to the rest of this atlas

The statute

Water Code §13181 asks the Board to keep SWAMP's needs assessment current. The last published needs assessment this atlas found dates from 2010. The three planned assessments would bring it up to date. Plate II

The budget

The funding framework is the program's own account of the history told in Plate IV, and its case for a more stable and varied funding base.

Who pays

Post-fire, plastics and Tribal monitoring have no obvious fee payer. They are the kinds of work Plate V assigns to general or broad-based funding.

The research

Asking what decisions the data must serve before designing the monitoring is what the literature in Plate VII recommends to avoid "data-rich, information-poor" programs.

The work that is not on the list

The plan is explicit that its 45 projects sit on top of the core workload. Keeping the program running each year means:

  • Planning and managing nine statewide contracts with laboratories, universities and partner agencies
  • Preparing the annual workplan and budget for the federal Clean Water Act §106 grant
  • Writing annual monitoring plans for each statewide program
  • Running the standing coordination meetings: the SWAMP Roundtable, the Safe to Eat and Bioassessment workgroups, and the Scientific Review Committee
  • Maintaining the program's public web pages and data resources
  • Hosting and mentoring California Sea Grant Fellows

What the plan does not contain. It does not restore the Bioaccumulation Monitoring Program or the Regional Water Boards' monitoring allocations described in Plate IV. Those depend on budget decisions outside the program's control.

Takeaway

For the next five years SWAMP plans to get its house in order, find out what its users need, and be ready with tested methods for wildfire, DNA-based monitoring, plastics and Tribal partnerships. Whether any of that becomes monitoring on the water depends on the funding questions in Plates IV and V.
