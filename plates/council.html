The Council: SB 1070 and the California Water Quality Monitoring Council

By 2006 dozens of agencies were sampling California's waters, each in its own way, and the public had no single place to see the results. Senate Bill 1070 (Kehoe) told the state's two environmental agencies to fix that by coordinating what already existed. It is now Water Code §13181.

The problem the Legislature described

The bill opens with findings. Three of them explain the whole statute.

"...the state can only report on the health of 22 percent of its coastal shoreline, 34 percent of its lakes and reservoirs, and 15 percent of its rivers and streams due to a lack of monitoring data. There is no single place where the public can go to get a specific look at the health of water bodies in its own backyard..."SB 1070, Section 1(d)
"State water board funding for ambient surface water monitoring has fluctuated significantly over the years, and is inadequate to ensure the assessment of all waters."SB 1070, Section 1(e)
"...there are 100 water quality monitoring efforts underway in the central valley alone, and coordination is minimal."SB 1070, Section 1(g)

What it built

Questions the public actually asks

The Council organized state data around plain questions: Is it safe to swim? Is it safe to eat the fish? Are our streams healthy? Each has a "My Water Quality" portal and a standing workgroup that crosses agency lines.

Shared plumbing

A common data exchange (CEDEN, public since 2010) so that data from state programs, local agencies and volunteers can be combined, plus agreed methods and quality standards so the numbers are comparable.

What the statute left open

No money

The Assembly Appropriations Committee expected "substantial" cost pressure of about $5 million a year. The bill carried none. The Governor signed it with this message:

"We should be working within the parameters of these enhanced financial resources and not increase fees on Californians."Governor's signing message, 2006

Eight years later a joint state and federal review found the Council had "essentially no dedicated funding, and relies mostly on voluntary contributions."

Advisory only

The Council recommends. It cannot direct an agency's budget or set standards, a point the 2020 memorandum makes explicit. Its 2010 strategy asked for four coordinator staff and about $10 million over ten years for data systems.

The audit

The statute calls for a CalEPA audit every three years. This atlas could not find a published one. That is a gap in our search, not proof none exists.

Takeaway

SB 1070 is a coordination statute. It assumes the monitoring itself is paid for somewhere else, mainly by SWAMP. When SWAMP shrinks, the Council has less to coordinate.
