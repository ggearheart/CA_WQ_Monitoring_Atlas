Plate VI

Benefits: what the research says monitoring is worth

Monitoring does not clean water. Its value comes from the decisions it improves. Researchers have measured that value in several ways. The studies below are peer-reviewed unless noted, and each number is taken from the paper itself.

1. It steers much larger spending

Cleanup is expensive. Monitoring is how an agency avoids spending on waters that are fine and missing waters that are not.

Finland · 144 lakes
Value exceeds cost

A study of lake management concluded that "generally, the value of monitoring exceeds the cost." The largest gains came from avoiding unnecessary restoration of lakes that already met standards.

United States · Clean Water Act
$1 trillion, evaluated

The first full evaluation of the Clean Water Act's results used 50 million readings from 240,000 monitoring sites. It found the share of waters safe for fishing rose 12 percentage points from 1972 to 2001. Without the monitoring record, a trillion dollars of public spending could not have been evaluated at all.

Netherlands · North Sea
Positive, if accurate

A formal value-of-information analysis found that adding satellite observation to water quality monitoring improves welfare, and that the gain depends on how accurate the information is.

2. It protects health at beaches and lakes

Southern California · 28 beaches
$21 to $51 million a year

Estimated cost of stomach illness from swimming at Los Angeles and Orange County beaches: 627,800 to 1,479,200 excess cases a year. Monitoring is how beach managers know when and where to post warnings.

United States · all surface waters
90 million illnesses

Recreational waterborne illness nationwide is estimated at about 90 million cases and $2.2 to $3.7 billion a year.

Utah · one lake, one bloom
$370,000

Health benefit from detecting a single 2017 toxic algal bloom earlier with satellite data, so that advisories went up sooner.

Lake Michigan · one beach
12% of days

Share of monitored days a beach was closed unnecessarily because lab results took 24 hours. Each closure day cost swimmers $1,274 to $37,030. Faster and better monitoring has a direct payoff.

3. It makes rules work

Independent measurement and public reporting change behavior, even without new enforcement.

Massachusetts · 517 water systems
30 to 44% fewer violations

Larger utilities that were required to mail water quality reports to customers cut total violations by 30 to 44 percent.

India · Gujarat
Honest numbers, less pollution

When auditors were paid by the plants they audited, readings clustered just under the legal limit. When auditors were made independent, reports became truthful and plants cut pollution.

Review of the evidence
Deterrence is real

A review of the empirical literature finds that environmental monitoring and enforcement produce substantial deterrence, both at the inspected facility and at its neighbors.

4. Long records find what no one was looking for

Acid rain in North America was first documented from long-term records at the Hubbard Brook research forest in New Hampshire, which were not started to look for it. Ecologists have made the case that this is typical: the largest payoffs from monitoring are often unplanned, and they require records that run for decades without gaps.

Monitoring is "a fundamental component of environmental science and policy," and its authors call for larger and steadier long-term funding. .

5. New tools add to field monitoring, not replace it

Satellites could save U.S. agencies an estimated $5.7 to $316 million a year in lake monitoring costs. The authors of that estimate are explicit that satellite and field monitoring are complements (). Satellites cannot measure toxins, bacteria, or what is in fish.

An honest caveat

Economists who have added up the measured benefits of U.S. water quality policy find them lower than its costs in most studies, and say the evidence is incomplete and likely undercounts benefits (). That is an argument for more measurement, not less. It is also a reminder that water quality improves well-being through many channels that are hard to count ().

Takeaway

Monitoring budgets are measured in millions. The decisions they inform, and the illness and damage they help avoid, are measured in hundreds of millions to billions.
