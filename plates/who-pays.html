Plate V

Who Pays: permit holders or the general public?

Two respectable principles point in different directions. "Polluter pays" says those who discharge to public waters should cover the cost of watching those waters. "Beneficiary pays" says everyone swims, fishes and drinks, so everyone should pay. California has tried both. This plate sets out the arguments, the history, and one way to reconcile them.

The two principles

Polluter pays

The Legislative Analyst's Office has described fees as a way to make those "who use or degrade a public resource (such as air, water, and wildlife) ... pay all or a portion of the social costs." It has recommended fee funding for the Water Boards' core programs since at least 2008.

  • Monitoring exists largely because discharges exist.
  • Ambient data is used to write and justify permits and cleanup plans.
  • Fees are steadier than the General Fund in a recession.
  • State law already allows it (Water Code §13260(d), §13181(f)).

Public good, beneficiary pays

The same LAO analysis says programs with a public-good character are properly paid from general taxes, "reflecting the collective benefit to the state's citizens," and that general funds are the practical choice where benefits are widespread.

  • Fish advisories and swim warnings serve everyone.
  • Much pollution comes from sources with no permit: farms, streets, old mines, wildfire, climate.
  • A city's sewer customers end up paying to study problems they did not cause.
  • Fee payers have a fair claim to ask what their fee buys them.

Sources: ; ; ; . The other side of the record is the Governor's 2006 message on signing SB 1070, which asked that monitoring be expanded without raising fees (Plate II).

Three wrinkles in the current arrangement

Not every fee payer contributes

Irrigated agriculture pays permit fees, but those fees do not fund SWAMP. Farm runoff is one of the main things ambient monitoring tracks.

The fee rules treat it differently

Federal facilities are generally not billed for the share of a fee tied to ambient monitoring. The fee schedule cites a U.S. Supreme Court case on when a charge is a fee for service and when it works like a tax.

Fees grew, monitoring did not

The water quality fee fund rose from $76 million in 2009-10 to $197 million in 2025-26. SWAMP's contract budget stayed at $5.8 million, then fell.

One way to reconcile them

The question "fee payers or the public?" assumes monitoring is one thing. It is several, and they have different beneficiaries. Matching each purpose to the principle that fits it gives a mixed answer. This is the atlas's reading of the principles above, not adopted policy.

A broad-based charge is not a new idea. In 2008 the LAO suggested a small fee on every water connection, "less than $10 per year," to replace General Fund support for cleanup plans and basin planning ().

Takeaway

The source of the money matters less than its stability. SWAMP once had a defined share of permit fees. It now has a discretionary line that did not grow with fees and was cut when budgets tightened. A stated share, from a mix of payers that matches who benefits, would answer both the fairness question and the stability question.
