An atlas in eight plates

California Water Quality Monitoring Atlas

California asks its Water Boards to protect every river, lake, estuary and beach in the state. To do that, somebody has to go out and measure them. This atlas explains why that job falls to government, the two state laws that organize it, what it costs, who pays, and what research from around the world says happens when it is done well or not done at all.

The atlas in five sentences

  1. Ambient monitoring is the regulator's own view of the water, independent of what permit holders report about their own discharges. Federal law makes it a condition of California's clean water grants.
  2. Two statutes shaped California's system. AB 982 (1999) asked for a comprehensive program and produced SWAMP. SB 1070 (2006) asked state agencies to coordinate and produced the Monitoring Council.
  3. Neither statute came with money. The Water Board told the Legislature in 2000 that a full program would cost $59 to $115 million a year. SWAMP has never had more than a small fraction of that.
  4. Since 2011 fee payers, not the General Fund, have carried the program. Its contract budget stayed at $5.8 million for two decades while prices rose 66 percent, then fell to $2.2 million.
  5. The research is consistent. Monitoring usually costs far less than the decisions it steers, and the expensive failures are the ones nobody was measuring.
